Good chocolate is surprisingly fragile. Heat melts it, moisture spoils its surface, and strong smells seep into it. The good news is that storing it well takes no special equipment: a cool cupboard and an airtight container do most of the work.

The four rules

Chocolate keeps best when it is cool, dark, dry and sealed. Aim for roughly 60 to 68 degrees Fahrenheit (about 15 to 20 Celsius), out of direct sunlight, away from the oven, the dishwasher and any sunny windowsill.

Keep it wrapped. The original foil or a tight wrap of plastic film, inside an airtight tin or container, protects it from humidity and from absorbing smells. Cocoa butter picks up odours readily, which is why chocolate stored next to onions or coffee can end up tasting of them.

Why the fridge is usually a mistake

A refrigerator is cold, but it is also humid and full of smells. The real problem comes when you take chocolate out. Warm room air condenses on the cold surface, the water dissolves a little sugar, and when it dries the sugar recrystallises as a dull, gritty white film called sugar bloom.

If your kitchen regularly runs hot in summer and the fridge is the only option, wrap the chocolate tightly, seal it in a container, and let it come back to room temperature inside the container before you open it. That keeps the condensation on the outside of the box rather than on the chocolate.

How long chocolate keeps

These are general guides for properly stored chocolate. Always follow the date and advice on the package or from the shop.

  • Dark chocolate bars: the longest-lived. Often a year or more before flavour starts to fade.
  • Milk and white chocolate: shorter, because milk fats go stale sooner. Usually several months to around a year.
  • Filled chocolates with shelf-stable centres such as pralines and caramels: typically a few weeks to a few months.
  • Fresh cream truffles and ganaches: the shortest. Many artisan truffles are best within one to two weeks. Ask the chocolatier, because recipes vary.
  • Chocolate-dipped fresh fruit: eat within a day or two and keep it chilled.

Can you freeze chocolate?

Yes, with care. Wrap it tightly, seal it in a freezer bag or container, and move it to the fridge for a day before bringing it to room temperature, still sealed. That slow staging avoids the condensation that causes bloom. Freezing works well for bars and baking chocolate. It is less kind to filled chocolates, whose centres can change texture.

What if it has already bloomed?

White streaks or a dusty coating are almost always bloom, not mould, and bloomed chocolate is generally safe to eat. The texture and snap suffer, but it melts perfectly well for baking, sauces or hot chocolate. Our guide to chocolate bloom explains the two kinds and how to tell them apart.

Buying for later

If you are buying chocolate as a gift or for an occasion a few weeks away, choose bars or shelf-stable pieces, and save fresh truffles for something you will eat soon. A good chocolatier will happily tell you how long each item in the case will keep.
